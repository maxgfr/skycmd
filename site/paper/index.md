# Pre-registered gates for small models trained on one laptop: what passed, what failed, and what the readings show

## Abstract

We trained 20 small models (143 KB to 20.7 MB) on one laptop and judged 15 of them by a gate written before training: a test read once (from s5 on, the evaluation tools refuse it before a listed hash or a marker file), every change a dated amendment, and every failure published with its file. Five passed, two of them on a second look, and ten failed. Four lessons follow, each from few lines and mostly one training seed. (1) Check a gate against trivial baselines before freezing it: answering each question with its most common training answer scores 0.4815 where the passing model scores 0.4805, and a BM25 bar added before training caught a router that only tied it. (2) Check early exits by running the model in its loop: thresholds fitted on held-out questions crashed one model in every Flappy episode on five seeds, and conformal thresholds (α = 0.02) raised another's safety vetoes from 23 to 1,016. (3) Hide or rename the input: masking tool names took the passing router from 0.8868 to 0.2497 while BM25 kept 0.8138, and a diagnosis traced the fall to its trained part's data, where the teacher had rewritten only the right tool's description. (4) Read each pass on data built differently: three of the five passes carry a limit measured there. Nine more versions stopped without a model, seven of them at a check their pre-registration named. Box 1 condenses the protocol and these lessons into a checklist for pre-registering a small-model experiment. The report is not peer-reviewed; the pre-registrations, result files and model files are public.

## 1. Introduction

A *decision model* receives a state (a game frame, a short text, the last seconds of audio) and one or more typed questions, and returns a probability for every allowed option in one forward pass, without generating text. The question types are `choice` (one of several options), `score` (an ordered level) and `noul` (a probabilistic yes or no) ([`docs/research.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/research.md)). This format, called "System One" by TypeSafe, which introduced it with its closed model Jev in September 2026 [[1]](#ref-1), is served by other open models from 706,048 parameters (cua-s1-forms, which answers one form-filling task [[2]](#ref-2)) and 10.4M (TinyDecide, fine-tuned from ELECTRA-small [[3]](#ref-3)) to 421M (Laya [[4]](#ref-4)), and by larger models up to 27B (Cloudflare's clef and openjev, listed in [`docs/research.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/research.md)); oscar-1-17m [[5]](#ref-5), on Ettin-17M, has about 18.5M parameters. The open models we found that cover the whole format start from a pretrained backbone ([What we bring, section 1](/novelty/#_1-a-decision-model-in-251-kb)).

skycmd asks how small such a model can be, and what it can be trusted with, when it is trained from scratch on one laptop. Its first answer was s3-pico, 445,345 ternary parameters in a 251,104-byte file, which plays four control games at the level of an exact oracle ([manifest](/models/manifest.json), [What we bring, section 1](/novelty/#_1-a-decision-model-in-251-kb)); among the open models we found that take their options at request time, it is the smallest file that accepts all three question types, and the only one with published ternary weights. Other systems are smaller or ternary under other conditions: Sparrow [[6]](#ref-6) compiles a network of about 2 KB per question fixed at build time (its general tiers start at 1.11 MB), blink [[7]](#ref-7) answers choices only, from 452 KiB of int8 weights, BitSOne [[8]](#ref-8) is ternary on 2,435,763,712 parameters with private weights, and the project's own s1-pico has fewer parameters (202,305) in a larger float16 file (545,280 bytes). Small is not good on free text: on `typed_decisions_bench` s3-pico scores 0.382, below the majority class (0.398) (section 6.1). The later lines, s4 to s6, carried the same engine to questions in free text, to sound, to tool lists given at run time, to secrets in code, to time series and to a fast-weight memory.

This report contributes four things.

1. **Artefacts.** Model files from 142,912 bytes (s6-cast-pico) to 3,692,288 bytes (the two files of s6-route7-nano) for the s6 family, and up to 20,684,640 bytes for s5-lite, each published with its card, its verdict and a demo that runs it in the browser from the file the numbers were measured on ([manifest](/models/manifest.json)). Every file is a GGUF container, but only the s1 files also run in llama.cpp and wllama; every other file uses an architecture that only the project's own Rust engine runs, natively and compiled to WebAssembly, from the same file (section 8).
2. **A protocol applied to every line from s5 on**, with the failures published as results: a pre-registration frozen before the computation it governs, a test read once, dated amendments, a checkpoint rule fixed in advance, and a second look named as such (section 3.2). Pre-registration is not new in machine learning (section 2); what is contributed is its application to every line, with the test locked by the tools.
3. **Lessons across lines** (section 5), each stated with its evidence and its limits. None of them was pre-registered as a test; they are patterns in the published readings.
4. **A checklist** (Box 1, section 3.2): twelve rules for pre-registering a small-model experiment, each with the reading that taught it.

Its claim to being a scientific result rests on the protocol, not on a leaderboard number. Several of the models are weak; the point is that how weak they are, and where, was fixed in advance and is checkable.

To use a file, start at [Use it for real](/use/): two files are usable with a safety net (s6-ear2-nano with a fallback timer; s6-route when tool names are distinctive, otherwise BM25). The companion methods report [[9]](#ref-9) says how the models are built and trained and what each design choice bought; this report holds the protocol, every gate as read and the lessons. The project's terms (*line*, *reading*, *slate*, *oracle*, the model names) are defined in Appendix A.

## 2. Related work

Every work cited here is also listed on the project's [Sources](/sources/) page, and each reference was checked against its arXiv or project page. The literature search behind the project was two research passes, not a systematic review ([What we bring](/novelty/#what-it-does-not-bring-honestly)). Two prior-art searches made in November 2026, after most lines had been read, found work those passes had missed; this version of the report credits it ([Sources](/sources/#related-work-found-later)), and none of it changes a verdict.

**Decision models.** TypeSafe's Jev (a closed API, 15 September 2026 [[1]](#ref-1)) launched the format; Laya [[4]](#ref-4) (421M parameters), Julia-1 [[10]](#ref-10) (144M) and oscar-1-17m [[5]](#ref-5) (about 18.5M) are open models in it, and llama.cpp serves it at `/v1/systemone` ([`docs/research.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/research.md)). Two smaller open models appeared in September and October 2026: cua-s1-forms [[2]](#ref-2), 706,048 parameters in a byte-level embedding and a 2-layer Transformer encoder of width 128, for one form-filling task, and TinyDecide [[3]](#ref-3), whose 10.4M-parameter build starts from ELECTRA-small. The project's s5 line builds on two papers about the format: one shows that typed decision models follow the option's name rather than its definition [[11]](#ref-11), and another reduces the answer's sensitivity to the options' order with a block-causal attention [[12]](#ref-12).

**Order invariance.** Answers that cannot depend on the options' order are adopted here, not contributed. Set-LLM [[13]](#ref-13) proves permutation invariance for a language model with set attention masks and set positions, and Amit [[12]](#ref-12) isolates the candidates from one another. Jeva [[14]](#ref-14) and Runoff [[15]](#ref-15) are concurrent work of October 2026: the first also makes the options' representations reusable across requests, the second needs no training and scores each option alone before comparing a few. What is the project's own is the exact test of these properties, to 1e-6 natively and in WebAssembly, in a sub-megabyte ternary model (section 3.1).

**Architecture.** The graph is described in the methods report [[9]](#ref-9). A small shared block looped with an answer after each loop follows TRM [[16]](#ref-16) and Ouro [[17]](#ref-17), after the weight sharing across depth of Universal Transformers [[18]](#ref-18) and the variable number of steps of ACT [[19]](#ref-19) and PonderNet [[20]](#ref-20); in typed decision models, LEV [[21]](#ref-21) loops layers instead of decoding tokens and stops each question with a small stopper whose threshold is fitted on unlabelled requests. A loss at every exit follows deeply-supervised nets [[22]](#ref-22), and skipping loops at random in training follows stochastic depth [[23]](#ref-23). One token per measured value follows FT-Transformer [[24]](#ref-24) and FoNE [[25]](#ref-25). Ternary weights trained in follow BitNet b1.58 [[26]](#ref-26), and their behaviour on small networks is studied by Nielsen and Schneider-Kamp [[27]](#ref-27) and by Nielsen et al. [[28]](#ref-28). The s5 backbone is Ettin [[29]](#ref-29), an encoder in the layout of ModernBERT [[30]](#ref-30). Frameworks such as Burn [[31]](#ref-31) also train in Rust and run in WebAssembly; what the project's own engine adds is the combination of ternary kernels, the looped graph with its exits and one file run natively and in the browser.

**Calibration, early exit and imitation.** Temperature scaling and the expected calibration error (ECE) with 15 equal-width bins are those of Guo et al. [[32]](#ref-32). Early exits go back to BranchyNet [[33]](#ref-33), and in language encoders to DeeBERT [[34]](#ref-34) and FastBERT [[35]](#ref-35). Early-exit thresholds with a guarantee come from Conformal Risk Control [[36]](#ref-36), a conformal method (surveyed by Angelopoulos and Bates [[37]](#ref-37)), in the early-exit setting of CALM [[38]](#ref-38) and Fast yet Safe [[39]](#ref-39); Learn then Test [[40]](#ref-40) underlies the s5 escalation experiment. Choosing exit thresholds against the task's own success was done by DeeR-VLA [[41]](#ref-41), which sets the exits of a robot vision-language-action model from demonstrations or, when the environment can be used, by Bayesian optimisation against task success under compute budgets (section 5.2). Imitation that holds up in closed loop uses DAgger [[42]](#ref-42). The models trained from a teacher (section 3.1) learn its soft labels, as in knowledge distillation [[43]](#ref-43).

**Closest work by line.** Each later line has close prior or concurrent work. *Secrets:* proposing candidates with rules and judging each with a learned model is the design of Samsung's CredSweeper [[44]](#ref-44), whose ML validator file is 11,545,911 bytes, of SAP's Credential Digger [[45]](#ref-45) and of GitGuardian's FP Remover [[46]](#ref-46), and GitHub's secret scanning now assesses candidate secrets in context with a ModernBERT classifier [[47]](#ref-47) (section 6.2). *End of turn:* a 1.14M-parameter end-of-turn model distilled from wav2vec 2.0 representations into an MFCC-based student [[48]](#ref-48), and turn-1-mini [[49]](#ref-49), a streaming system of 6,098,317 learned parameters; Sharon et al. [[50]](#ref-50) compare end-of-turn detectors trained on human-human conversation and on single-turn human-machine speech (section 6.4). *Tool routing:* semantic-router [[51]](#ref-51) routes a request by its embedding's similarity to each route, and OATS [[52]](#ref-52) moves tool embeddings, offline, toward the requests where each tool succeeded; ToolRet [[53]](#ref-53) benchmarks retrieval over a corpus of 44,453 tools ([`docs/s6-route4-study.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/s6-route4-study.md)), a larger task than choosing among at most seven (section 6.3). *Forecasting:* Fracast-0 [[54]](#ref-54) has 85,001 parameters and reports a better GIFT-Eval score than s6-cast2-pico (section 6.5).

**Pre-registration and published failures.** Neither is new in machine learning. NeurIPS held workshops on pre-registration in 2020 [[55]](#ref-55) and 2021 [[56]](#ref-56); Hofman et al. propose pre-registration for predictive modelling [[57]](#ref-57); a three-month programme reports pre-registered negative results on language-model compression [[58]](#ref-58), and Modgil reports pre-registered tests of learned fluid representations, each read once on held-out data [[59]](#ref-59); and RSI-Jev publishes every failed version of its typed-decision models [[60]](#ref-60). What this report adds is the application: every line from s5 on, with the test locked by the evaluation tools and every change a dated amendment, and all 20 models published, failures included (section 3.2). The controls of section 5.3 follow hypothesis-only baselines [[61]](#ref-61) and choices-only baselines [[62]](#ref-62), with the caution of Feng et al. that a partial-input baseline can fail to reveal a shortcut [[63]](#ref-63).

## 3. Setting and protocol

### 3.1 The models and the training setting

**The models in brief.** The decision models share one graph, the project's own, implemented twice in Rust: for training with mlx-rs on Apple Silicon, and for inference, natively and in WebAssembly; golden tests tie the two ([`crates/sky-infer/tests/golden_s2.rs`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/crates/sky-infer/tests/golden_s2.rs)). The question and its options are encoded once and cached, and no option attends to another, so an answer cannot depend on the options' order, a property adopted from prior work (section 2); in the s5 iso format the option keys are not rendered either, so renaming them cannot change an answer. These properties are checked by exact tests to 1e-6, natively and in WebAssembly; they are tests, not experimental results ([Results, s5](/results/#what-is-guaranteed-by-construction-s5-iso-architecture)). Each measured value is one token, so a Flappy frame takes 9 tokens instead of 16 ([`BENCH.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/BENCH.md), section "Inference"). A few shared blocks are looped with an exit after each pass: an early exit answers at the first pass whose top probability exceeds a threshold τ, and τ = 1 means full depth. Weights are ternary (−1, 0 or +1 times a scale), trained in after a float warm-up and stored at 2 bits. The end-of-turn models and the forecasters use the same blocks in graphs of their own. The methods report [[9]](#ref-9) describes all of it precisely enough to reimplement it, with the recipe of each file and what each choice bought. Intervals in this report are 95% bootstrap intervals over test items (questions, clips or slates), or over test repositories for the secrets verifier.

Every model was trained on one Apple M5 laptop with 16 GB of memory, the machine its owner works on ([`docs/big-run-report.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/big-run-report.md)). A *brake* lets training run hard when the machine is idle and gently when it is in use, on battery or hot: a daemon writes a duty cycle and a work-and-rest pattern to one file, and the training loop rests between steps. The rests stay out of the run's clock, so every budget is counted in **working minutes** and computes the same thing in every mode; only the wall-clock duration changes ([`docs/idle-training.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/idle-training.md)). The project ran one budgeted GPU run at a time.

The training data are public sets under their own licences, game oracles written in Rust, and labels from open-weight teachers. The teacher of s6-nano and s6-micro, GLM-5.3-flash [[64]](#ref-64) (open weights, MIT licence), labelled 747,077 of the 747,198 selected training questions through its provider's API; the 121 others were refused by the provider's moderation ([`docs/big-run-report.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/big-run-report.md) §2 and §7). The end-of-turn student was distilled from Smart Turn v3.2 [[65]](#ref-65) (BSD-2-Clause) and the forecasters from TinyCast [[66]](#ref-66) (Apache-2.0) ([Results](/results/#s6-ear2-nano-passed), [`docs/big-run-report.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/big-run-report.md) §5). Every source and its licence is listed in [`DATA_LICENSES.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/DATA_LICENSES.md).

### 3.2 The protocol

The rules below are those of [`AGENTS.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/AGENTS.md), section "Pre-registered experiments", applied from s5 on; [`docs/README.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/README.md) indexes every pre-registration with its status.

- **Pre-registration.** Before a model is trained, a file in `docs/` fixes the data and its splits, the model and its budget, the test it will be read on, the gate (a set of conditions that must all hold), what is reported without being gated, and what a failure would mean. It is committed before any computation it governs. There are 24 such files (a count made here of `docs/*prereg*.md`).
- **A test read once.** A test set is opened once, by the step the pre-registration names. For s5, the evaluation tool refused the test split of a frozen set unless the pre-registration listed the set's hash, and logged every pass over it ([`docs/s5-prereg.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/s5-prereg.md)). In later lines the evaluation entry points refuse the test until a marker file (`TEST-OPEN`, or `FRESH-OPEN` for fresh files) is created after the validation steps; for s6-route-nano and s6-route that file was created under the owner's standing delegation, not by the owner's hand, and the result blocks say so ([`docs/s6-nano2-prereg.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/s6-nano2-prereg.md), [`docs/s6-nano3-prereg.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/s6-nano3-prereg.md)).
- **Dated amendments.** Any change is an amendment, dated and committed before the computation it concerns; it says what had been seen when it was written and what does not change. Amendments include technical ones (a memory cap, a resumed run) and decisions of the owner (publishing failed files, closing a line).
- **The checkpoint rule is fixed in advance.** For example the last DAgger round at full depth for s6-nano, or the exponential moving average of the weights for s6-cast-pico and s6-ear3-nano; no intermediate checkpoint is chosen by looking at it ([Results](/results/#s6-november-2026)).
- **No rerun to see.** Only a technical failure justifies a new run, and it is recorded. Two examples: s6-nano's decision training was stopped by the memory cap at step 16,020 and resumed from its checkpoint at step 15,573; s6-micro v2's died on a file-access error and was resumed from step 61,051 ([`BENCH.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/BENCH.md), section "skycmd-s6").
- **Failures are published as results.** Since the owner's decision of 15 November 2026, a model that fails its gate is published with its file, a card that opens with the failure and a manifest entry marked `gate: failed`; since 17 November 2026 each has a demo page (a Hugging Face Space) ([`AGENTS.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/AGENTS.md)).

**What the history shows, and what it cannot.** Appendix A gives, for every pre-registration, the commit that froze it and the first commit that records its reading (Table A1). The dates are those the commits declare, with no outside time-stamp; s5's pre-registration, its amendments and its results entered `main` in one squashed commit, and the freezes up to 5 November 2026 are squash merges of pull requests. Nine pre-registrations were written in French and translated into English after their readings, so the frozen texts are the French originals, and the big run's result files were committed five days after its gates were first recorded (Appendix A).

Four terms are used throughout this report.

- **Second look.** A system designed after a test was read. When it is judged on the same records, its result is not an independent test: this is the case of s6-micro v2 (a pass) and s6-secrets v3 (a failure). When it is judged on records built afterwards, the test is new but the design is not blind to the earlier reading: this is the case of s6-route, whose fusion was first seen on s6-route-nano's already-read test. All three are called second looks wherever they appear below.
- **In-sample for design.** A test set whose readings shaped a later version's design choices, though never its weights: Smart Turn's test clips for s6-ear2-nano and s6-ear3-nano, GIFT-Eval for s6-cast2-pico ([Results, limits](/results/#status)).
- **Reported, not gated.** A number the pre-registration announced but that decides nothing.
- **Exploratory.** A reading made outside any pre-registration: the closed-loop comparison of s2-nano's thresholds, and the studies and diagnoses written after a gate from files already read. None of them changes a verdict, and each is named as such where it is used.

::: info Box 1. Checklist for pre-registering a small-model experiment
Each rule is one this project applied or should have applied, with the reading that taught it.

1. **Before freezing a bar, score the trivial baselines on validation**: the majority class, each question's most common training answer, and a lexical or linear model of the same size; set the bar above the best. s6-nano's bar was the majority class (0.398), and the most common training answer scores 0.4815 against its 0.4805; s6-route-nano's BM25 bar, added before training, caught a router that only tied it (section 5.1).
2. **Rehearse the bar in the gate's own metric and split**; do not import a published number. A margin set with Gitleaks' published 0.442, a span F1 on another split, met a Gitleaks that reads 0.7037 in line F1 on the gate's test (section 5.1).
3. **Reproduce the baseline's published number through your harness before opening the test.** s6-ear-nano's bar was first read as 0.8518 through a padding bug; s6-ear2-nano's harness was checked against the published 0.9263 before any test score was read (Table 1 and section 6.4).
4. **Write whether the verdict reads the point or the bound, and which unit is resampled.** s6-micro-v1 failed on a point ECE of 0.0821 whose interval [0.0746, 0.0900] holds the bar of 0.08; s6-route's +0.0698 over BM25 is [0.0455, 0.0952] when slates are resampled and [−0.0079, +0.1404] when clusters are (Table 1).
5. **Pre-register data checks that can stop a version before training**, such as: no rule that ignores the request beats chance. They stopped s6-route v4, v5 and v6 (Table 2).
6. **Pre-register controls that hide the input or rename the options.** The routers' collapse under renamed tools was found after their gates (section 5.3).
7. **Lock the test in the tool**, with a marker file or a hash that the evaluation entry points check, and log each pass (section 3.2).
8. **Fix in advance the checkpoint rule, the rerun rule and what a failure will mean** (section 3.2).
9. **Read once on data built differently, with an untrained reference read on both sides.** BM25 itself fell by 0.2494 from s6-route's held-out tools to a new tool family (section 5.4).
10. **Measure the seed spread before setting a tolerance.** One Dodge veto decided two exit thresholds, where the vetoes spread by ± 4.1 over five seeds, and the ablation's gains of 0.0051 to 0.0081 sat beside seed deviations of 0.0033 to 0.0043 (sections 5.2 and 6.7).
11. **Name every second look**: judged on the same records, on fresh records, or on a test in-sample for its design (section 3.2).
12. **Commit each freeze alone, never squashed with its results**: the history cannot show the order of s5's freeze and reading (Appendix A).
:::

## 4. Every gate, as read

### 4.1 Counts

The counts below are made here from the manifest of published files ([`manifest.json`](/models/manifest.json)), the status table of [Results](/results/#status) and the index of [`docs/README.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/README.md).

- **Published models: 20**, the entries of the manifest. s6-route has no model file of its own: it reuses `s6-route-nano.gguf`, with the same sha256, next to its embedding file. Gate passed: 5 (s6-nano, s6-micro v2, s6-secrets-pico, s6-ear2-nano, s6-route). Gate failed: 10 (s4-micro, s6-micro-v1, s6-ear-nano, s6-ear3-nano, s6-cast-pico, s6-cast2-pico, s6-secrets-verify-pico, s6-route-nano, s6-route7-nano, s6-sleep3-nano). No gate: 5 (s1-pico, s2-nano, s2-nano-doom and s3-pico, built before the project fixed gates, and s5-lite, a longer run of the iso format declared outside the s5 bets for the demo, by amendment 3 of its pre-registration). The pre-registered s5-lite gates, read on the model its rule chose (seed 0 of the usual format), failed (fitted bench 0.541, English demo cases 50%), and s5's bet 3 ran as an exploratory reading (amendment 4) ([Results, s5](/results/#s5-october-2026)).
- **Of the 15 gated models**, 14 had a pre-registration document frozen before training; s4-micro had a gate fixed before training and recorded in `BENCH.md`, with no pre-registration document ([`docs/README.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/README.md)).
- **Second looks: 3.** s6-micro v2 (passed, same records), s6-route (passed, fresh records, design after a test reading) and s6-secrets v3 (failed, same records; not a new file).
- **Versions closed without a model: 9.** Eight had a pre-registration. Seven of them stopped at a check or rule it named: s6-sleep v1 (its pilot), v2 (a CPU check) and v4 (two checks on training sequences); s6-route v4, v5 and v6 (data checks, before any training); the LFM3 fine-tuning (no method fitted the machine's memory bound). The eighth, four flexible-weights experiments under one pre-registration, was closed without running after a literature review. A ninth, a possible s6-secrets v4, was closed by a diagnosis and had no pre-registration.

## 5. Lessons across lines

The lessons below were not pre-registered as tests. Each one is a pattern in readings already published, stated with the lines it rests on and what it does not show; Box 1 turns them into rules.

### 5.1 A gate must be checked against trivial baselines before it is frozen

- **s6-nano.** The bar was the majority class over the whole bench (0.398). Answering each question with its most common training answer, a rule that reads no state, scores 0.4815, above s6-nano's 0.4805; a size-matched linear model reaches 0.703 on the validation split of the same tasks. Both were measured after the gate ([Results](/results/#s6-nano-what-the-gate-did-not-measure)). A gate set against the per-question prior would have failed.
- **s6-route-nano.** Here the check happened before training: amendment 1 added BM25 to the gate when BM25 beat the planned embedding baseline on validation. The gate then caught a router that ties a lexical score: −0.0105 [−0.0265, +0.0055] against BM25, where the planned bar on the embedding baseline, potion-base-2M + 0.03, was cleared (0.8395 against 0.6975) ([Results](/results/#s6-route-nano-failed)). BM25 stayed above the retrained router on the new tool family (section 6.3).
- **s6-secrets-verify-pico.** The margin of +0.15 was set with Gitleaks' published 0.442 in mind, a span F1 on 168 CredData test repositories [[67]](#ref-67); on the gate's test, Gitleaks reads 0.7037 in line F1 ([Results](/results/#s6-secrets-verify-pico-failed)). A bar set from a published number, rather than rehearsed on the line's own validation data, ended up harder than intended.
- **Limits.** Three lines. In one the trivial baseline was found too late, in one it was found in time, and in one the bar moved the other way. Other gates did include a trivial baseline from the start (the silence timer for end of turn, 0.4979; the seasonal naive for forecasting).

### 5.2 Early exits should be checked where the model is used

An early exit lets a looped model answer after fewer passes when it is confident. Two models suggest the same lesson; it rests on them, not on a test The rules that chose and checked the thresholds, with their algorithms, are in the methods report [[9]](#ref-9), section 5 ([What we bring, section 3](/novelty/#_3-a-lesson-calibrate-early-exits-where-the-model-is-used)).

**Table 3.** Early-exit thresholds fitted away from the model's own play against thresholds checked by playing. s2-nano and s3-pico: 5 arena seeds (7, 11, 13, 17 and 19) × 30 episodes, replayed at fixed thresholds by `cargo xtask loopcal`, mean ± standard deviation over seeds ([`loopcal.json`](/results/loopcal.json); Doom is played by s2-nano-doom). s6-nano and s6-micro v2: one run of 30 episodes per game on seed 7, the seed and episodes their conformal thresholds were fitted on ([`BENCH.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/BENCH.md), section "skycmd-s6", Arena). Units: Flappy pipes are the mean per episode (at most 200), crashes the episodes that crashed, Rescue the mean number of hikers rescued per episode (of 3), Dodge vetoes the reflex's interventions summed over the 30 episodes of a seed, and mean passes the block passes per decision.

| model and thresholds | Flappy pipes (of 200) | Flappy crashes (of 30) | Rescue | Dodge vetoes | mean passes |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| s2-nano, τ fitted on held-out questions (0.62 / 0.62 / 0.69) | 15.86 ± 2.30 | 30 ± 0 | 2.98 of 3 | 1,931.4 ± 1,105.8 | 1.32 (Flappy) |
| s2-nano, τ = 0.95 fixed for play | 191.94 ± 7.16 | 2.4 ± 2.1 | 3.00 of 3 | 250.4 ± 505.7 | 3.43 (Flappy) |
| s3-pico, τ = 0.6 chosen by playing | 200 ± 0 | 0 | 3.00 of 3 | 9.6 ± 4.1 | 1.44 (Flappy) |
| s3-pico, full depth | 200 ± 0 | 0 | 3.00 of 3 | 9.6 ± 4.1 | 4 |
| s6-nano, full depth | 200 | 0 | 3.00 of 3 | 23 | 4 |
| s6-nano, conformal thresholds (α = 0.02) | 200 | 0 | 2.80 of 3 | 1,016 | 1.01 to 1.83 per game |
| s6-micro v2, full depth | 200 | 0 | 3.00 of 3 | 20 | 6 |
| s6-micro v2, conformal thresholds | 200 | 0 | 3.00 of 3 | 21 | 2.0 to 2.7 of 6 |

- **Evidence.** s2-nano, at thresholds fitted on held-out validation questions, which come from the oracle's play and not the model's, crashed in every Flappy episode on all five seeds; the same weights at τ = 0.95 flew 191.94 pipes on average. s6-nano's thresholds came from conformal risk control, fitted on the decisions it met while playing 30 episodes per game at full depth on seed 7: they bound at α = 0.02 how often an early answer differs from the full-depth answer on such decisions. Played at those thresholds, on the same seed and episodes, Rescue fell to 93% (2.80 of 3) and the Dodge vetoes rose from 23 to 1,016, so the pre-registered rule rejected them and the file ships at full depth. The same rule accepted s6-micro-v1's conformal thresholds, at which every game kept its mean score (its gate then failed on calibration at both depths), and rejected s6-micro v2's by one veto, 21 against 20, a difference smaller than the seed-to-seed spread of the Dodge vetoes measured for s3-pico (± 4.1 over five seeds) ([Results](/results/#s6-micro-v2-passed-on-a-second-look); [`BENCH.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/BENCH.md), section "skycmd-s6", Arena).
- **Why it can happen (a hypothesis, not measured).** Playing with early exits changes which states the model visits, so a guarantee fitted on held-out questions, or on full-depth play, need not cover the states its own early decisions produce: conformal guarantees assume exchangeable data, which interaction can break [[68]](#ref-68). Even without such a shift, a per-decision rate adds up over an episode: at α = 0.02, an episode of 21,300 Flappy decisions, the length of s3-pico's full-depth episodes ([`arena-baseline.json`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/runs/s6-sleep3/gate/arena-baseline.json)), allows on average 426 early answers that differ from full depth (computed here); and in imitation learning, errors that take a policy away from the expert's states can compound quadratically with the horizon [[69]](#ref-69).
- **Prior work.** Tuning exit thresholds against task success in closed loop is not new: DeeR-VLA [[41]](#ref-41) sets the exits of a robot policy from demonstrations or by Bayesian optimisation against task success, and reports both working in closed loop. These readings are consistent with that line of work, and add a case where thresholds fitted away from play failed badly: s2-nano crashed in every Flappy episode, on one model and five seeds.
- **Limits.** The evidence is two models where the fitted thresholds failed in closed loop: s2-nano, in an exploratory run, not a pre-registered test, and s6-nano, under a pre-registered rule read once, on one seed and one 30-episode run per game. For s3-pico the check confirmed that its exits were safe rather than rescuing anything; s6-micro-v1 kept its scores at its conformal thresholds, and s6-micro v2 lost none. Every threshold was chosen and checked on seed 7, and only the s2-nano and s3-pico thresholds were replayed on other seeds. All of it is on four friendly games whose useful features are computed by code.

### 5.3 Controls that hide or rename the input expose shortcuts

- **Renamed options.** Masking tool names as `tool_<k>` took s6-route-nano from 0.8395 to 0.1050 and s6-route from 0.8868 to 0.2497, where BM25 kept 0.8445 and 0.8138; the diagnosis traced it to the teacher's rewritten description marking the right tool in every renamed training slate (section 6.3). s6-route7-nano, trained without that cue, lost only 0.0170 under masking ([Results](/results/#s6-route7-nano-failed)).
- **The request hidden.** With the request replaced by `?`, a router that reads the request should be at chance. The published s6-route files read 0.1104 to 0.1402 on validation and fresh slates, at or below a chance of 0.1429 ([Results](/results/#s6-route-passed)), but on T5, the new tool family, s6-route reads 0.1794, with an interval over servers of [0.1466, 0.2138] (reported): above chance, though under the bar that v7 was held to ([Results](/results/#s6-route-passed)); s6-route7-nano's trained part read 0.1406, but its pair read 0.1969, above the bar of 0.1929, because the untrained embedding already did (0.1931).
- **Rules that never read the request.** Counts made with no model found that the right tool's description is the slate's shortest far more often than chance: a rule picking it is right on 0.2439 of s6-route-nano's test slates whose answer is a tool and on 0.3633 of s6-route's fresh ones, against 1/7; whether the models used it is not measured ([study](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/s6-route4-study.md)). Pre-registered data checks of the same kind stopped versions 4, 5 and 6 before any training (Table 2).
- **Controls that passed.** For s6-ear3-nano, the label cross-check (0.9509) and the probe showing the silence did not carry the label (AUC at most 0.4396) held before training, and the version still failed on its gate (section 6.4).
- **Prior work.** Hiding part of the input is a standard control: hypothesis-only baselines exposed annotation artefacts in natural language inference [[61]](#ref-61), and choices-only prompts show that language models can answer multiple-choice questions without the question [[62]](#ref-62). The request-hidden reading here is the same control for a router, and, as Feng et al. warn [[63]](#ref-63), a router at chance under it can still use a cue together with the request.
- **Limits.** All the shortcut findings come from one line, tool routing, and most were found after its gates. The rules count what a cue could buy, not what a model used.

### 5.5 What the negative results are

Three results are negative in the sense that an addition did not do what it was built for.

- **The ablation** adopted none of its eight additions at the pico size (section 6.7). This says that none brought 0.01 of validation accuracy there, under these budgets; it does not say they cannot help elsewhere.
- **The memory is used, and sleep adds nothing measurable.** s6-sleep3-nano's memory gains +0.2452 on `recall`, its sleep passes +0.0030 with an interval holding zero, and the memory costs two games (section 6.6). One probe, one size, three seeds.
- **The System 1 / System 2 router keeps accuracy but not the token budget**, because the answer without thinking is not cheap enough for routing to pay (section 6.7). One model, maths questions only.

## 6. Line by line

This section gives, line by line, the numbers the tables cannot hold. Each subsection opens with its takeaway.

### 6.1 Decisions and games

**Takeaway.** A 715,136-byte model plays four games at the oracle's score at full depth, but its gate on typed questions in free text was too low to show more than a per-question prior.

**s6-nano** is the s3 architecture at language scale, trained with AdamW after the ablation of section 6.7 kept none of its additions. Its pretraining read 0.87B tokens, 588 per parameter ([`BENCH.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/BENCH.md), section "skycmd-s6"). On `typed_decisions_bench`, the test split of `LocalLLaMA/typed-decisions` [[70]](#ref-70) (2,000 questions), it scores 0.480, above the majority class (0.398), where s3-pico (0.382) and s4-micro (0.352 on the same records, 0.343 in the older record layout its gate was read on) stayed below; s5-lite reaches 0.696, and oscar-1-17m 0.6775 by the figure of its model card, 0.682 by the project's own reading on s5's rendering of the same 2,000 questions ([Results](/results/#s6-nano-passed-published-at-full-depth); [`BENCH.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/BENCH.md), section "skycmd-s5"). It plays the four games at the oracle's score at full depth: Flappy 200 of 200 pipes with no crash, Rescue 100%, Dodge 87% with 23 reflex vetoes, the Doom-like game 100% of levels. It fails on what it never trained on: 0.055 on the 13-way subject question of NOTAM, a set of aviation notices (chance 0.077), and 0.404 on When2Call [[71]](#ref-71) ([`docs/s6-nano2-study.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/s6-nano2-study.md) §1, §3.4).

The gate was too low, and a study made after it shows why ([Results](/results/#s6-nano-what-the-gate-did-not-measure)). On 17 of the bench's 20 tasks, s6-nano gives one answer to at least 95% of the items; its 0.4805 equals answering each question with its most common training answer (0.4815). The bench's tasks were 0.4% of its decision-training draws. A logistic regression on hashed character n-grams, no larger than the 715,136-byte file, reaches 0.703 on the validation split of the same tasks: a rehearsal on validation, not a test score. English MASSIVE [[72]](#ref-72), an intent-classification set held out from training, is not strictly zero-shot (206 of its 2,000 test utterances are also in training; amendment 14 of [`docs/s6-gen-prereg.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/s6-gen-prereg.md)). The verdict does not change.

**s6-micro v1** (5,587,201 parameters) scored 0.547 on the bench but missed the ECE guard: 0.0821 at full depth, 0.0804 at its conformal thresholds, against 0.08. It is calibrated in distribution (ECE 0.037 on validation), and one zero-shot NOTAM statement, 412 of the 11,000 test items, weighs up to about 0.02 of its pooled test ECE ([amendment 12](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/s6-gen-prereg.md); [What we bring](/novelty/#at-a-glance)). **s6-micro v2**, with twice the decision-training minutes and everything else the same, passed the same gate on the same test records, 0.553 with an ECE of 0.0588; it is a second look ([Results](/results/#s6-micro-v2-passed-on-a-second-look)).

**Before s6.** s4-micro, distilled from Qwen3.5-4B in one night, scored 0.343 against a gate of 0.55 ([`BENCH.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/BENCH.md), section "skycmd-s4"). The three pre-registered bets of s5 all missed (Table 2). The first was the iso format of section 3.1: at equal data it was far more accurate than the usual format on the fitted bench (0.674 against 0.429, +0.245 [+0.136, +0.321], three seeds each) and less well calibrated, by more than the margin fixed in advance. The coherence losses of bet 2 improved only the identities they were trained on, 8-bit quantization could not be shown non-inferior for lack of power, and the online escalation of bet 3 failed in all six cells ([Results, s5](/results/#s5-october-2026)).

### 6.2 Secrets

**Takeaway.** A detector trained on the project's synthetic texts is not a scanner of real files; judging the candidates that rules propose beat every rule scanner on real repositories, by less than the margin its gate asked.

**s6-secrets-pico** (the s3-pico graph, 250,272 bytes) reads a short text and answers four questions. On `secret_bench`, 1,996 synthetic texts in four file types the training never shows, it scores 0.9990 on "is a usable secret exposed?" and 0.9795 on "which service issued it?", against 0.977 and 0.787 for a gitleaks-style rule scanner on the same texts; its line answer is weak (0.6859, ECE 0.3051) ([Results](/results/#s6-secrets-pico-passed-published-at-full-depth)). The bench is synthetic: our generator's formats and templates, with fake keys. Measured after publication on 719 real files in which a rule scanner finds nothing, cut into 22,433 chunks, it flags 10.6% of the chunks and 59.8% of the files at p ≥ 0.9, and every flag is a false positive; its recall on real secrets was not measured ([real text](/results/#s6-secrets-real-text)). The gate stays passed; the model is published as the result of its pre-registration, not as a scanner.

**s6-secrets-verify-pico** changes the task: a rule proposes a candidate value and the model judges it in context. This is the design of CredSweeper, Credential Digger, FP Remover and GitHub's classifier of candidate secrets (section 2); what is new here is the size, 249,984 bytes against the 11,545,911 bytes of CredSweeper's validator, and the comparison on test repositories the model never trained on. Trained and tested on Samsung's CredData [[73]](#ref-73), on 221 test repositories its line F1 is 0.8561, against 0.7332 for the best rule scanner (Betterleaks 1.10.1), +0.1229 [0.0413, 0.3613] where the gate asked for +0.15. Two learned baselines were read on the same test: a logistic regression of the same size reads 0.7945, though it matched the verifier on the validation repositories (0.8604 against 0.8603), so the verifier's edge over it appears only on the test repositories; and cbx-secrets-detector-17M (67 MB), rerun out of sample, 0.6830 on every repository and 0.8493 without the largest repository, where the model reads 0.8114. Six gates of seven hold, among them 31 of 8,825 clean real files flagged (0.35%) and an ECE of 0.0073 ([Results](/results/#s6-secrets-verify-pico-failed)). The bar of +0.15 was set with Gitleaks' published 0.442 in mind, a label-agnostic span F1 on 168 CredData test repositories from the card of cbx-secrets-detector-17M [[67]](#ref-67), a different metric on a different split; on this test Gitleaks reads 0.7037 in line F1. Rules and verifier together (s6-secrets v3), a second look on the same files, read 0.8477 and failed G1 again ([Results](/results/#s6-secrets-verify-pico-failed)). A diagnosis made afterwards found that most missed API lines never reach the verifier, because the candidate generator proposes nothing on them ([`docs/s6-secrets4-diagnosis.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/s6-secrets4-diagnosis.md)).

### 6.3 Tool routing

**Takeaway.** No trained router beat BM25 on its own; multiplying a trained model with a static embedding did, on fresh requests, while tool names stay distinctive.

A router reads a request and up to 7 tools, each as `name: description`, plus *none of these tools fits*. Routing by embedding similarity is the method of semantic-router, which OATS refines (section 2). **s6-route-nano** (the s6-micro graph, chosen by its pilot rule) also reads each tool's BM25 [[74]](#ref-74) score (the Lucene variant of BM25S [[75]](#ref-75)) in its state, because amendment 1, written before training when BM25 beat the embedding baseline on validation, added BM25 to the gate. On 2,000 test slates of tools never seen in training it reaches 0.8395, above the static embedding potion-base-2M [[76]](#ref-76) with cosine (0.6675) and both trained baselines of its size, but tied with BM25 (0.8500), and it takes 38.9 ms per request in WebAssembly where 2 ms was asked. With every tool renamed `tool_<k>` it falls to chance (0.1050) ([Results](/results/#s6-route-nano-failed)).

**s6-route** trains nothing: it multiplies, option by option, the probabilities of that file and of potion-base-2M packed to 4 bits, raised to 0.81 and renormalised, and asks the user below a top probability of 0.671047. Its fusion was first seen on s6-route-nano's already-read test, so it was pre-registered with parameters from validation and read once on fresh requests: a second look on fresh records. On 945 fresh requests about the held-out tools it reaches 0.8868, against 0.8169 for BM25 and 0.9048 for all-MiniLM-L6-v2 [[77]](#ref-77) (91.6 MB), with an ECE of 0.0162 and 30.9 ms per request in WebAssembly; every condition held. Alone, its model part reads 0.7926 and its embedding part 0.7386 on the same requests ([`runs/s6-nano3/eval-fresh/results.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/runs/s6-nano3/eval-fresh/results.md)). Stated with the pass: the 60 ms latency bar was set after s6-route-nano was measured; the advantage over BM25 leans on the three largest clusters (without them +0.0202 [−0.0062, +0.0481]), and a bootstrap that resamples the 273 clusters instead of the slates gives +0.0698 [−0.0079, +0.1404]: both intervals hold zero; with names masked it falls to 0.2497 ([Results](/results/#s6-route-passed)).

A teacher that rewrites only the right option teaches the student to spot the rewrite. A diagnosis made after the gate, on validation files only, explained the masked-name collapse that way: in training, the right tool of every renamed slate was the only option whose description the teacher had rewritten, and *none* was never right there, so under `tool_<k>` names the model part picks the description that reads most like a rewrite. On validation, giving one distractor the rewritten description makes the model pick it on 0.8311 of the slates ([`docs/s6-route4-diagnosis.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/s6-route4-diagnosis.md) §1). Versions 4, 5 and 6 tried to rebuild the data without such cues and each stopped at its data checks before training (Table 2). **s6-route7-nano** trained on the rebuilt data and was read once on T5, 2,000 fresh requests about the tools of 262 MCP servers from Toucan [[78]](#ref-78), a family no training slate holds. With the request hidden its trained part is at chance (0.1406 against 1/7), and masking the names costs the pair 0.0170; but the pair reads 0.5610 against 0.5675 for BM25, falling from 0.8802 on validation, with an ECE of 0.1845, and with the request hidden it picks the right tool on 0.1969, above its bar of 0.1929, as its untrained embedding part alone already does (0.1931) ([Results](/results/#s6-route7-nano-failed)). Why it falls from validation to T5 is not measured. The line is closed; s6-route stays the published router, and reads 0.5530 on T5 (reported).

### 6.4 End of turn in speech

**Takeaway.** A 631,936-byte student of Smart Turn passes on Smart Turn's test clips and needs a fallback timer on real speech; retraining it on human conversations did not carry over to speech addressed to agents.

**s6-ear-nano** tried three tasks on one audio encoder pretrained to predict the tokens of the Mimi codec [[79]](#ref-79). It failed three of its four gates (end of turn 0.5681 against 0.9263 for Smart Turn v3.2), and its control without the pretraining did better on all four sets ([Results](/results/#s6-ear-nano-failed)).

**s6-ear2-nano** drops the pretraining and trains one task for 240 working minutes as a student of Smart Turn v3.2. On the 31,527 test clips of Smart Turn v3.2 it answers 0.8993, with a false cut-off of 0.0847 and an END recall of 0.8627 at its validation threshold τ₈₅ = 0.7244; the teacher reads 0.9260 through the same harness, the silence timer 0.4979, and the student takes 2.39 ms per decision in WebAssembly from a 631,936-byte file ([Results](/results/#s6-ear2-nano-passed)). Its harness was checked against Pipecat's published 0.9263 before any test score was read. Stated with the pass: the test set is partly in-sample for its design choices, and on eot-bench [[80]](#ref-80), LiveKit's 17,934 labelled pauses of real speech addressed to agents, it and its teacher are far weaker (AUC 0.7299 against 0.7838); at τ₈₅ its false cut-off there is 0.2230 and its recall of finished turns 0.5253, where the teacher, at its own threshold of 0.5, reads 0.3678 and 0.7998 ([`BENCH.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/BENCH.md), section "s6-ear2-nano"): the student cuts fewer speakers off and misses about half of the real ends of turn. It is not the smallest end-of-turn model described: the model of Helwani et al. [[48]](#ref-48) has 1.14M parameters, against 1,306,178. What is its own is a published 631,936-byte file, the only ternary end-of-turn file we found, distilled from Smart Turn and run in WebAssembly.

**s6-ear3-nano** keeps that graph and trains it again for 120 working minutes, half on real two-speaker English conversations of the gated otoSpeech corpus [[81]](#ref-81), each pause labelled from the other speaker's channel. The labels were checked before training: a second detector, Silero VAD [[82]](#ref-82), agreed with them on 0.9509 of the validation records (bar 0.85), and the silence after a pause did not carry the label by itself (AUC at most 0.4396, bar 0.60). On eot-bench, read 0.2 s into each pause (16,230 cuts), its AUC is 0.7197 against v2's 0.7448, −0.0252 [−0.0336, −0.0167] where at least +0.02 was asked; on 113 held-out conversation sessions (reported; session-disjoint, not speaker-disjoint, and with no interval computed) it reads 0.6878 against v2's 0.6031 ([Results](/results/#s6-ear3-nano-failed)). By the reading its pre-registration fixed before the run, the human-to-human signal was learned and does not transfer to speech addressed to agents ([`docs/s6-ear3-prereg.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/s6-ear3-prereg.md) §12, §17). That reading is of this recipe, one training run and these two sets. On other corpora, Sharon et al. [[50]](#ref-50) find that end-of-turn detectors trained on human-human conversation beat those trained on single-turn human-machine speech, by up to 0.45 in F1, and that a test on single-turn speech alone hides that benefit; the reading here does not test their claim. No statement about otoSpeech goes beyond these published counts and statistics, and no record derived from it is published.

### 6.5 Forecasting

**Takeaway.** Neither forecaster reached its bar; the project's forecaster is its port of TinyCast.

**s6-cast-pico** applies the encoder to patches of 32 points of a series and outputs nine quantiles for each of the next 64 steps. On GIFT-Eval [[83]](#ref-83) (97 configurations, every score divided by the seasonal naive's, lower is better) it reads nGMASE 0.9471 and nWQL 0.6646, against bars of 0.838 and 0.587 taken from the training-free FLAIR [[84]](#ref-84) as the TinyCast paper reports it; TinyCast as published [[85]](#ref-85) reads 0.774 and 0.545 ([Results](/results/#s6-cast-pico-failed)). **s6-cast2-pico** adds TinyCast's zero-parameter period detector, a seasonal draft in each decoder query, sub-hourly real series and 720 working minutes. It improves on v1 (0.8847 and 0.6190), and its MASE is under the seasonal naive's at every term (G2 holds), but it misses G1 and stays behind the project's TinyCast port (0.7918 and 0.5622 without flip); on Chronos-ZS [[86]](#ref-86), which its design never read (reported), it reads 0.9014 and 0.7143 against 0.8598 and 0.6928 for the port ([Results](/results/#s6-cast2-pico-failed)). GIFT-Eval is partly in-sample for its design. Its file is 148,512 bytes against the port's 595,840, and it forecasts 64 steps in 838 µs natively against 21,531 µs, on one synthetic series and one core. The line stops; the project's forecaster is the port. Found later, Fracast-0 [[54]](#ref-54) (85,001 parameters, 26 September 2026) reports MASE 0.808 and WQL 0.564 on the same 97 GIFT-Eval configurations: it is smaller than s6-cast2-pico and, by its abstract, better, and TinyCast as published stays ahead of both. No reading changes.

### 6.6 Memory

**Takeaway.** The fast-weight memory is used, the sleep passes add nothing measurable, and the memory costs two of the four games.

s6-sleep adds a fast-weight memory (a small matrix written once per frame and read by every later frame) and "sleep" passes, after Lee et al. [[87]](#ref-87), that replay the last frames. Version 1 stopped at its pilot and version 2 at a CPU check (Table 2). **s6-sleep3-nano** holds the memory's gates at their initial values and adds, in training only, a loss that decodes the asked colour from the memory. On the `recall` test (a door's colour shown once, asked 1 to 32 frames later; chance 0.25) it is right on 0.4892 of the questions against 0.2440 for the same training without a memory, three seeds pooled, and the gain fades with the delay (0.6147 at 1 to 4 frames, 0.3302 at 17 to 32). Four sleep passes add +0.0030 [−0.0010, +0.0068], and the memory costs two of the four games: Flappy 15.87 against 71.05 for the control, Dodge 5.89 against 7.94; the control itself flies far fewer Flappy pipes than s6-nano (71.05 against 200) ([Results](/results/#s6-sleep3-nano-failed)). The gate is read on the three seeds pooled; the published file is seed 0, which alone reads 0.4765 on `recall` ([`BENCH.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/BENCH.md), section "s6-sleep3-nano"). Version 4 failed both checks on training sequences and is closed (Table 2).

### 6.7 The LFM3-1.7B router and the ablation

**Takeaway.** Check a cascade's arithmetic before running it, and size an ablation's threshold against its seed spread.

**System 1 / System 2.** For Liquid AI's LFM3-1.7B [[88]](#ref-88), a language model that can answer with or without thinking, a 6-coefficient probe on the answer given without thinking (System 1) decides whether to ask again with thinking on (System 2), at a threshold from conformal risk control. On 1,000 test questions of GSM8K [[89]](#ref-89) and MATH-500 [[90]](#ref-90), a subset of MATH [[91]](#ref-91), with the 1,024-token budget chosen on calibration, System 1 alone scores 0.667 at 559 tokens, System 2 alone 0.796 at 958, the router 0.786 at 1,032 and the oracle 0.814 ([Results](/results/#lfm3-s1s2)). The router keeps the accuracy (H2) but uses more tokens than always thinking (H3 fails): System 1 alone already takes more than half of System 2's tokens, and every question sent on pays for both. The check could have been made before the run: System 1 already costs 559 of System 2's 958 tokens, more than the 479 tokens that half of 958 allows (computed here), so no router that asks System 1 first could have met H3.

**The ablation.** Before the big run, a pre-registered ablation at the pico size decided which of eight additions to adopt; each needed a validation gain of 0.01 (0.005 for the training options) with no game lost. The additions were Muon [[92]](#ref-92), in a recipe after Liu et al. [[93]](#ref-93), Parallax attention [[94]](#ref-94), exclusive self-attention [[95]](#ref-95), the SODA weight decay of Pethick et al. [[96]](#ref-96), exit distillation after SAFE-KD [[97]](#ref-97), a bias-corrected weight average [[98]](#ref-98), StableQAT [[99]](#ref-99) and a pretraining curriculum [[100]](#ref-100); the ablation was designed knowing that gains over AdamW shrink once AdamW is tuned as carefully [[101]](#ref-101) and that, at equal time, AdamW can beat Muon on small language models [[102]](#ref-102). None was adopted: Muon gained 0.0051 and lost Flappy score, Parallax attention 0.0054, exclusive self-attention 0.0081, and SODA weight decay, exit distillation, a weight average, StableQAT and a pretraining curriculum each lowered validation accuracy (by 0.0021 to 0.0062) ([`runs/recipe/recipe.json`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/runs/recipe/recipe.json); [What we bring](/novelty/#at-a-glance)). Two limits go with this reading. The seed-to-seed standard deviations of the arms compared by the first three rules, three seeds each, are 0.0033 to 0.0043, the order of the gains measured. And the five isolated changes were read against Parallax attention with Muon, a base that the first two rules did not adopt, so their effect on the adopted AdamW base was not measured (same file).

## 7. Limitations

- **One training run per gated model.** Except s5 (three seeds per format) and s6-sleep3-nano (three training seeds, pooled), every gated model was trained once, on one seed. The one line read on two training seeds, s6-sleep v4, met its last bar on one seed of the two ([Results](/results/#status)).
- **Intervals are over test items, not over training runs.** They say how much a number would move on other test items, not how much it would move if the same recipe were trained again.
- **Own benchmarks and friendly games.** s6-secrets-pico's bench is the project's own generator. The games compute the useful features (relative clearances, an A* waypoint) and leave the model to decide, and the useful baselines there were not run: a same-size MLP on the same features, an RL policy, oscar-1-17m on the games ([What we bring](/novelty/#what-it-does-not-bring-honestly)).
- **Second looks and in-sample designs.** s6-micro v2 and s6-secrets v3 were judged on test records already read; s6-route was designed on a test already read; Smart Turn's test is partly in-sample for s6-ear2-nano and s6-ear3-nano, and GIFT-Eval for s6-cast2-pico.
- **Gates that were too low or too loose.** s6-nano's gate is passed by a constant answer per task (section 5.1); s6-route's latency bar was set after its predecessor was measured.
- **Baselines not run.** For s5, the baselines B1 (label log-probability through the MLM head) and B3 (a linear head on the frozen backbone) were not run, a deviation declared before any result ([Results](/results/#not-done-yet)). Ablations of s3 over several seeds and a standard control benchmark with vector states were not done.
- **The literature search was not systematic**: two research passes, then two prior-art searches in November 2026 that found work the first passes had missed (section 2).
- **Earlier models have no gate.** s1-pico, s2-nano, s2-nano-doom and s3-pico were built before the protocol; their numbers are measurements, not gated results.
- **One project, one machine, no outside review.** The protocol was written and applied by the same project: nobody independent froze the pre-registrations, opened the tests or checked the amendments, and some test-opening markers were created under the owner's standing delegation rather than by the owner's hand, as the result blocks state. Nothing here has been peer-reviewed.
- **The lessons of section 5 are post hoc.** They were assembled after the readings and are not tests.

Every manifest entry also records the sha256 of its file, or of each of its two files, and the site's tests check each one against the committed file ([`site/tests/manifest.test.ts`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/site/tests/manifest.test.ts)); several result blocks record the same hash for the file they published (for example amendment 17 of [`docs/s6-gen-prereg.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/s6-gen-prereg.md) for s6-micro and §17 of [`docs/s6-ear3-prereg.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/s6-ear3-prereg.md) for s6-ear3-nano). - **Formats and runtimes.** Every file is a GGUF container. Only the s1 files, which use llama.cpp's `modern-bert` architecture with the `laya` decision head, also run in llama.cpp and wllama, where their probabilities agreed with the engine's within 4.7e-4 ([`docs/compat.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/compat.md)). Every other architecture (`skycmd-s2` for s2 to s6, `skycmd-iso` for s5, `skycmd-turn`, `skycmd-cast`, and `skycmd-embed` for the router's embedding) is read only by the project's Rust engine, natively and compiled to WebAssembly (a SIMD128 build and a relaxed-SIMD one); on 200 bench questions the browser's probabilities differ from the native ones by at most 9.5e-7 for s6-nano and 1.2e-6 for s6-secrets-pico ([`BENCH.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/BENCH.md), sections "skycmd-s6" and "s6-secrets-pico").
- **Hugging Face.** Each of the 20 published models has a model repository `maxgfr/<name>` whose card opens with its verdict, and a Space of the same name; the Space [`maxgfr/skycmd`](https://huggingface.co/spaces/maxgfr/skycmd) gathers every demo ([`hf/spaces.json`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/hf/spaces.json)). The 16 data sets are listed on [Sources](/sources/#our-datasets-on-hugging-face). Since 20 November 2026, [`maxgfr/skycmd-decisions`](https://huggingface.co/datasets/maxgfr/skycmd-decisions) holds both splits of `LocalLLaMA/typed-decisions` (`typed_decisions_train`, and `typed_decisions_bench` for evaluation only), and [`maxgfr/skycmd-glm-labels`](https://huggingface.co/datasets/maxgfr/skycmd-glm-labels) all 747,077 GLM-5.3-flash labels, marked as generated by a model.
- **What is not redistributed, and why.** Records derived from the gated otoSpeech corpus, whose access is granted per user; sets of real credentials (CredData's values are obfuscated upstream, and its files keep their own projects' licences); the s6-ear audio features (31 GB, rebuilt from their public sources); and evaluation sets used as given, such as GIFT-Eval ([Sources](/sources/#our-datasets-on-hugging-face), [`DATA_LICENSES.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/DATA_LICENSES.md)).
- **Commands.** The README's table "Reproduce a result" gives, for each s6 model, its script and result files ([`README.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/README.md#reproduce-a-result)); for example `scripts/train-turn.sh` for s6-ear2-nano or `scripts/route3-read.sh` for s6-route, which trains nothing. The scripts read their data from a folder next to the repository, described in [`docs/data-inventory.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/data-inventory.md). The figures of this report are rebuilt by `python3 -I scripts/paper-figures.py`.
- **Hardware.** One Apple M5 laptop with 16 GB of memory, under the brake of section 3.1; latencies were measured on the same machine, often while another job ran, as each result says.

## Appendix A. Terms, counts and the commit history

### A.1 Terms

The project's lines of models are named s1 to s6, in the order they were built: s1 a small encoder in Laya's layout, s2 the project's own two-stream graph with an exit after each layer, s3 the same graph with one shared block looped and one token per measured value, s4 a student of Qwen3.5-4B, s5 a model on the Ettin-17M backbone in an order-invariant format, and s6 the family trained in November 2026. The suffixes pico, nano and micro name sizes: 202,305 to 445,345 parameters for the pico files, 1,306,178 to 1,611,505 for the nano files and 5,587,201 for micro, except that s6-route-nano and s6-route7-nano, named in their line's sequence, use the micro graph ([manifest](/models/manifest.json)). A *line* is one task with its successive versions (s6-route v2 to v7, for example). A *reading* is one pass of a model over an evaluation set; a *gate* is the set of conditions that a pre-registration fixes for a reading, all of which must hold; a *second look* is defined in section 3.2. In the games, the *oracle* is the exact planner that labels the training data and sets the reference score, and a *reflex veto* is the coded safety reflex of the Dodge game taking over from the model. In forecasting, *flip* averages a forecast with that of the sign-flipped series, at twice the cost. In tool routing, a *slate* is one request with the tools offered for it. The *owner* is the author, who holds the repository and took the decisions named as the owner's; the *lead* and the run's *operator*, named in some result blocks, executed steps under the owner's standing delegation.

### A.2 The status table of Results

The status table of [Results](/results/#status) has 17 rows: 5 passed, 11 failed, 1 stopped by its pilot. Its 11 failures are 9 published s6 files, the s5 bets and the System 1 / System 2 router of LFM3-1.7B, which trained no weights. The table covers the pre-registered lines from s5 on; s4-micro, whose gate has no pre-registration document, is named in a sentence under it.

### A.3 What the history shows, and what it cannot

Table A1 gives, for every pre-registration, the commit that froze it and the first commit that records its reading. They come from `git log --follow` on each document and from `git log` on the line's result folder under `runs/`: the commit that adds the gate file, or the marker the pre-registration names (`TEST-OPEN`, `FRESH-OPEN` or `TEST-READ`). Three limits apply. First, the dates are those the commits declare: git does not certify them, and no outside party time-stamped a freeze. Second, s5's pre-registration, its three amendments and its results entered `main` in one squashed commit, `f426e53` of 14 October 2026 (the document says it was frozen on 13 October), and the log of its passes over the test, `runs/s5-test-log.jsonl`, is not committed; more generally, the commits that froze the pre-registrations up to 5 November 2026 (from `f426e53` to `6a3631b`) are squash merges of pull requests, so the order of the work inside each is not in the history. Third, nine pre-registrations (those of s5, s6, s6-gen, s6-ear, s6-secrets, s6-cast, the two of LFM3, and flexible weights) were written in French and translated into English on 19 November 2026, after their readings ([`docs/README.md`](https://github.com/maxgfr/skycmd/blob/main/docs/README.md)): the linked files are those translations, and the frozen texts are the French originals at the commits of Table A1; the translation commits name `1f180a5` as holding the last French versions.
